Voters punished the man who taxed their prices. Europe must answer tariffs with one voice

Section 301 lets the US president tax European trade with no vote by lawmakers, and the election changed nothing about that power. Every government that cuts its own deal spends the strongest card Europe holds.

7 November 2026

American voters judged the price of everything and punished the man who taxed it, yet the power to tax European trade survived the election untouched. Donald Trump lost both congressional majorities by wider margins than expected, and in the same week European defence shares fell, with BAE Systems down 5.2 percent and Rheinmetall down 3.2 percent, even as the Euro Stoxx 50 rose 0.8 percent. Who sits in Congress no longer decides who pays the tariffs, and Europe must stop negotiating as twenty-seven separate shoppers.

The vote gives us the mandate. CBS News exit polling found 89 percent of voters called the cost of living a major or crucial factor, and about two-thirds said Trump's tariffs had hurt the economy. He imposed those tariffs on his own voters first, and his approval fell to 38 percent, historically low for a president at midterms. That is the strongest card Europe holds: a president punished at the ballot box for taxing imports will not keep European goodwill for free, and every government that waits to cut a separate deal spends that card for him. Euronews reports Congress will probably not end the tariff threat, because the tariffs now sit under Section 301, which lets the president act without any vote by lawmakers.

The ballot box will not do the job for us. James Smith, an economist at ING, did not rule out new tariffs aimed at Europe and warned that a divided Washington could push the president to rely more on such powers. A Senate majority of 54 seats with two races still open changes nothing about that power. The Hill reported that the defeat was wider than expected, giving Democrats a real chance of curbing his power. He told CBS News he believed Democrats would want to work with him and that he did not want to spend his final two years rejecting their legislation, but he posted a picture of a red "veto" cap nonetheless. Because he must bargain with a hostile Congress on everything else, the one power left fully his becomes more tempting, and Europe is its standing target.

A confrontational common trade line risks escalation with our largest export market, some argue, and a Democratic Congress will moderate the president anyway, so Europe should wait and negotiate quietly, member state by member state. But Congress holds no vote over the president's power to tax imports, as Euronews has reported, so moderation requires a lever Washington's legislators do not hold here. Waiting helps only the man with the pen, and about two-thirds of voters in an election-day survey told pollsters his tariffs had hurt the economy.

The Council must act before the next tariff lands. ECFR warned before the vote that Trump would keep the power to tax imports in full even under a Democratic Congress, and that his last two years could be more volatile, not less. Give the European Commission one exclusive mandate to negotiate with Washington, and agree in advance that any new Section 301 tariff meets common counter-measures binding on all member states, so no minister signs alone and no factory town pays alone. The money to shield household bills and energy prices from that fight must be carried jointly, by common borrowing and fair taxes, not by each capital's cuts. The midterms showed what voters do to a man who taxes their bills.
