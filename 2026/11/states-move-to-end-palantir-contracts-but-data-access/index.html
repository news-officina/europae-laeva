States move to end Palantir contracts, but data access stays a vendor promise

The head of the Bundeswehr's cyber troops says Maven is not an option for Germany, and courts and governments across Europe have ended or set end-dates on Palantir work in recent years, with contracts there and in the UK worth more than €1.4 billion. The Bundeswehr is testing about 30 European systems, and the challenger's claim of full sovereignty is so far untested in public.

12 November 2026

Maven, the military intelligence system of the American software company Palantir, is not an option for Germany at the moment, Thomas Daum, head of the cyber troops of the Bundeswehr, the German armed forces, said. The call came in an interview with Handelsblatt.

It is "unthinkable" that employees of the company would have access to military data, Daum said, as quoted in German by Süddeutsche Zeitung.

On November 11, 2026, the European defence technology company Helsing unveiled Clarity, an agentic intelligence platform for military intelligence.

A capability this critical must be sovereign, said Gundbert Scherf, co-founder and co-head of Helsing. With Clarity, he added, European armed forces get software built in Europe that runs on their own infrastructure and stays under their control, as quoted in German.

European countries and institutions hold Palantir contracts worth about €264 million, and the United Kingdom adds £992 million, taking the total above €1.4 billion.

Last September, more than 120 NGOs, unions and institutions from 30 countries published an open letter asking their governments to cut their ties with Palantir, citing dependency and human rights violations.

The first break in court

The move away from Palantir did not begin with a company pitch. The first break came in court. In 2023, Germany's Federal Constitutional Court ruled police use of Palantir's automated data-mining unconstitutional, striking down Hamburg's law and forcing Hesse to rewrite its own.

In 2025, Germany excluded Palantir from a major Bundeswehr military-cloud project, and its domestic intelligence service chose the French firm ChapsVision instead. France's domestic intelligence service, the DGSI, is making the same switch.

As of July 2026, the Netherlands' defence ministry said it wants to stop using Palantir's platform within two years. Spanish government sources confirmed in October 2026 that the contract for the Armed Forces Intelligence Center, CIFAS, expires at end-2026 and will not be renewed. A Spanish company is to develop the alternative.

At the start of October, the United Kingdom announced a similar decision to end or replace its relationship with Palantir.

Palantir still leads the market

Palantir leads the market for such military intelligence systems: its Maven system sits in the Pentagon's and NATO's workflows and has been refined over years of use, Spiegel and The Decoder reported.

Helsing can point to the Ukraine war, where the company delivers drones, as one of the few European providers with operational experience, The Decoder reported.

On the launch, Helsing said data, models and operations remain under the customer's control. Clarity, it said, belongs to no other actor, as quoted in French and translated from its site.

In the Iran war, the US military ran Maven with Anthropic's Claude, which proposed and prioritised hundreds of targets and recommended armament. The US hit around 1,000 targets in the first 24 hours and more than 3,000 within days, the Wall Street Journal reported.

Military representatives warn of an attitude inside that work, the Wall Street Journal reported: "The computer said to do this".

In spring 2026, the US military almost boarded a Chinese ship, CNN reported. A chatbot in an intelligence report had falsely claimed it carried parts of a nuclear programme. "AI makes it possible to get to a bad idea faster", a source said.

Palantir rejects the case

Palantir rejects the case against it. A spokesman for the company said its software had proven itself on the battlefields in Ukraine. He called it the operating system of NATO and the world's leading military power.

A Palantir spokesman said the software is fully sovereign because no one, not even Palantir itself, can access a customer's data without that customer's involvement.

A Palantir spokesman did not oppose European states building their own providers. On the contrary, he said, they would be right to support the industrial development of their own competitors in this field over the coming 15 years.

The warning from the Palantir spokesman was about the timetable. "If they mix that with the sovereignty debate, they risk being unprepared for the national security threats the continent faces in the next five years", he said, as quoted in German by the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung.

A promise, not a property

Incumbent and challenger both claim full sovereignty, and the challenger's claim is so far untested in public. NATO chose the American system because no European one existed.

So sovereignty here is not a property of the software. It is a promise each vendor makes about its own conduct, checked only by the states that buy.

The Bundeswehr is testing about 30 European systems, including Clarity, and wants a prototype by mid-2027 so deployment can start the year after, Süddeutsche Zeitung reported.

Helsing representatives stayed vague on some questions, such as whether the company runs its own data centres, and Scherf would not say whether the Bundeswehr or other NATO armies are already testing Clarity.
